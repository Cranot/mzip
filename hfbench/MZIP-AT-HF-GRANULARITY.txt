WHY EVERY PRIOR NUMBER NEEDED RE-MEASURING
--------------------------------------------
xet-core compresses PER CHUNK. From source:
  xet_core_structures/src/xorb_object/constants.rs
      TARGET_CHUNK_SIZE = 64 KiB, min 8 KiB (div 8), max 128 KiB (x2)
      MAX_XORB_BYTES = 64 MiB (xorbs aggregate ~1024 chunks)
  xorb_object/xorb_chunk_format.rs
      XorbChunkHeader { compressed_length[3], compression_scheme: u8,
                        uncompressed_length[3] }   <- scheme is PER CHUNK
  xorb_object/compression_scheme.rs
      enum { None=0, LZ4=1, ByteGrouping4LZ4=2, Auto=99 }
      Auto -> BG4Predictor::bg4_recommended(): per-byte-position popcount
      histograms (4 x 9 bins), KL divergence of each position vs the pooled
      histogram (+1 base count per bin), recommend BG4 if max_kl > 0.02.
      lz4_flex FrameEncoder at default (FAST) level.
A 10 GB safetensors is ~160,000 independent ~64 KiB compressions. File size
never widens the window. So every "mzip vs HF" figure measured on 8 MiB slices
(the 15.84% headline) was NOT the drop-in comparison.
Reimplemented the exact predictor: it chooses BG4 on 100% of weight chunks at
64K for all 7 slices, and on 0% of text chunks. Forced bg4-lz4 is therefore
HF's true baseline on weights. Python `zstandard` vs `zstd` CLI parity checked:
+4 bytes (frame flag) at every level.

FIXED 64 KiB CHUNKS APPROXIMATE CDC (mean 64 KiB); the whole-file column is the
old comparison, kept for reference.

  handles 2-byte interleaving natively).

- bg4+mzip beats bg4+zstd-19 on EVERY slice (+1.9% to +7.7%), aggregate +3.55%.
- A dtype-agnostic "always bg4+mzip" policy is safe: it never loses to plain.

WHAT THIS DOES TO THE POST
---------------------------
- "mzip is 15.84% smaller than what HF stores" is NOT a drop-in claim. The
  drop-in numbers at 64K are: plain mzip +13.43%, bg4+mzip +17.49%.
- HF can take +6.2% (zstd-1) or +14.5% (zstd-19) themselves with a stock
  library, and ~+3% more with a per-dtype dictionary. mzip's defensible edge
  over their BEST stock option is +3.55% (bg4+mzip vs bg4+zstd-19) on
  safetensors -- and +10% on GGUF F16, where stock options are weak.
- HF's scheme does nothing on GGUF. That is the finding most useful to them.

RESULT 14 — THE zstd-1 CLAIM IS NOT UNIVERSAL (2026-09-02)
------------------------------------------------------------
On the 256 MiB gpt2 F32 slice at 64K: bg4-lz4 1.0925x, bg4-zstd-1 1.0385x
(-5.20% vs HF). Per-region probe (16 x 64K, start/mid/late), consistent:
   lz4 1,048,944 | bg4-lz4 ~990K (1.06x) | zstd-1 PLAIN ~973K (1.077x) |
   bg4-zstd-1 ~1,040K (1.008x) | bg4-zstd-3 ~1,019K | bg4-zstd-19 ~898K (1.167x)
Byte-plane entropies of this slice: 7.999 / 7.969 / 2.754 (22 distinct) / 7.999
-> the exponent byte is in plane 2: the range request landed 1 byte off 4-byte
alignment (300,000,000 / 200,000,000 offsets are not aligned to tensor start).
bg4, BWT and mzip's SoA are position-invariant so their ratios are unaffected.
MECHANISM of the zstd-1 failure: zstd codes literals with ONE Huffman table per
block; concatenating a 22-symbol exponent stream with three ~8-bit streams gives
every literal ~8 bits, erasing the entropy gain, and zstd-1's 5-byte fast hash
finds fewer short repeats than lz4's 4-byte hash. zstd-19 is unaffected.

RESULT 45 — WHY XET STORES F16 RAW, AND WHY WE DO NOT (the mechanism behind the biggest new win).
Per-chunk scheme choice measured with the Rust harness (none/lz4/bg4 counts out of 128 chunks):
  bloom-560m F16    bg4-lz4 1.0000 -> Auto stores RAW on 127/128
  llama2-7b  F16    bg4-lz4 1.0002 -> RAW on 127/128
  sdxl-unet  F16    bg4-lz4 1.0001 -> RAW on 127/128
  whisper    F16    bg4-lz4 1.0263 -> RAW on 89/128, bg4 on 39
  qwen2.5-1.5b BF16 bg4-lz4 1.1344 -> bg4 on 128/128 (same for tinyllama, weights-bf16)
Cause, measured with numpy (order-0 entropy of the high byte plane, distinct symbols, and the
density of repeated 4-byte words -- LZ4's minimum match):
  dtype  high-plane H0   symbols   4-byte-repeat density (high plane)
  BF16   2.68-2.76 bits    26-29     0.985-0.986   <- LZ4 finds matches everywhere
  F16    5.46-5.85 bits   108-125     0.204-0.244   <- LZ4 finds almost nothing
BF16 = 1 sign + 8 exponent + 7 mantissa, so the high byte is sign+7 exponent bits: 26-29 distinct
values, 98.6% of 4-byte words repeat, LZ4 wins. F16 = 1 sign + 5 exponent + 10 mantissa, so the high
byte carries sign + 5 exponent bits + TWO MANTISSA BITS: 108-125 distinct values, only 20-24% of
4-byte words repeat. The mantissa bits destroy the literal repeats LZ4 needs while leaving the
distribution highly skewed -- which an order-0 coder still captures. Order-0 bound of the two planes:
bloom 1.1829x, llama2-7b 1.5243x, sdxl 1.1885x, whisper 1.1555x; the plane coder achieves 1.1789 /
1.5073 / 1.1837 / 1.1661 -- i.e. within 0.3-1.1% of the bound.
=> Every F16 model on the Hub (SDXL, SD1.5, Whisper, Llama-2, Bloom, most F16 diffusion) is stored
UNCOMPRESSED today, and an order-0 byte-plane coder takes 12-24% off it. This is the cleanest
unclaimed class found in the whole programme, and it is a consequence of the format, not of tuning.

## What is structural and will not convert

1. **Anything against a memcpy.** On F16, GGUF and parquet Xet picks "None" and stores the chunk
   raw at 13-22 GB/s. Our own memcpy floor on the same bytes is 20-33 GB/s, which confirms that is
   what it is. Entropy decoding is 84-98% of our decode time on those files and the coded planes are
   95-100% of the bytes, so there is no overhead left to remove. The loss is the price of the ratio
   win (llama2-7b 1.0000 -> 1.5073), not an inefficiency.
2. **Compress throughput where we compress harder.** On repo JSON the bare codec with no framework
   at all runs 746.6 MB/s against Xet's 775.4 — zstd-1 is simply not faster than LZ4 at equal work.
   On the whisper tokenizer the bare zstd encoder is 370.9 MB/s against Xet's 496.7 while delivering
   1.38x its ratio. Matching Xet's compress speed there means compressing less: zstd --fast=3 gives
   536 MB/s at ratio 2.6133, beating Xet on both axes but surrendering 75% of the ratio win.
3. **q5_K_M's 4.17%** is above its own order-0 ceiling of 1.0464. Conditioning the quant symbol on
   its sub-block scale reaches further but needs ~38 KB of context tables per 64 KiB chunk to save
   ~0.2 KB.

## Two findings that corrected us, both against our own interest

* **The dictionary buys no ratio.** Under a repo-grouped held-out split the HF-trained dictionary
  gives 10.4844 versus stock's 10.5649. The +12% first measured was sibling-file leakage from an
  alternating split, where config.json and tokenizer_config.json from the same repo landed on
  opposite sides. Its real value is that it lets the whole ensemble be dropped at constant ratio.
* **The whisper tokenizer loss was a router bug, not a coder weakness.** mzip decides "text" by
  requiring 85% printable ASCII in the first 4 KiB, but HuggingFace tokenizer.json encodes
  byte-level BPE through the GPT-2 byte-to-U+0100 map, so every non-Latin vocabulary entry is a
  2-byte UTF-8 sequence and whisper's chunks measure 75.8-84.5%. Twenty of its 38 chunks failed the
  gate and were sent to the float-plane coder.

RESULT 65 — LoRA merges are LOW RANK, and recovering the factors beats coding their bytes by 3-7x [M]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2026-09-03, lowrank_probe.py, pairs/lowrank-*.json. A LoRA merge is exactly W' = W + B A with B (d x r),
A (r x k), so the difference D = W' - W has rank r before it is rounded to BF16. Every coder in this
programme treats D as a byte stream and codes its entropy. This asks whether the generative structure is
recoverable from the published bytes alone: SVD of D, then for each rank r reconstruct, round to BF16 and
count the elements reproduced EXACTLY. Factors are stored too, at f32 / int16 / int8 with per-column and
per-row scales; the product is formed in float64 so a decoder reproduces it from the stored integers.
The slice is a contiguous run of complete tensor rows, and any row subset of B A still has rank <= r.

RESULT 67 — sparse and constant regions hit huff0's one-bit floor: up to 94% of those chunks is
recoverable, on a narrow class [M]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The one slice with order-1 structure is gpt2 F32: mean 44%, max 94%, on 50% of its chunks. Cause: the
slice is 37.7% zero bytes and its chunks are 88-99% a single symbol — GPT-2 stores its attention mask as

run-length plane. Classes it would serve beyond GPT-2: pruned and structured-sparse checkpoints, masked
MoE experts, padded embedding tables.

The class splits by what the format actually holds: an INT8 or FP8 tensor whose values follow the weight

SUB-BYTE PLANE SEPARATION: measured and DEAD, with the reason (null result).
The architectural objection was that our coder splits into BYTE planes and an 8-bit element is one byte,
so nothing is split. Four alternatives were measured per 64 KiB chunk over the same slices, in bits per
element:
  dtype     raw bytes  nibble split  field split (E4M3 sign+exp | mant)  bitshuffle
  F8_E4M3     6.5169      6.7051              6.5638                      6.8850
  I8          4.5910      5.0515              5.0515                      7.9630
  U8          7.6183      7.6254              7.6254                      7.8868
Every split is WORSE than coding the byte. The reason is not empirical but structural: for a one-byte
element the 256-symbol alphabet already models the JOINT distribution of sign, exponent and mantissa,
and splitting the fields codes them independently, discarding the dependency between them. The
"conditional" column came out exactly equal to the byte column, as the chain rule requires
(H(byte) = H(exp) + H(mant|exp)) -- an identity, not a measurement, and a useful check that the probe
was arithmetically right. Bit-plane transposition (bitshuffle, as used by HDF5/blosc) is far worse still:
it destroys the symbol alphabet without gaining any context.

Consequence: on 8-bit data, byte-level order-0 IS the ceiling, and the only thing that could beat it is a
context model across elements, which RESULT 66 measured at 0.00% for weights. The class is therefore
finished at the ratios above -- but those ratios are pure gain, because Xet takes nothing.

RESULT 75 — one quantisation predicts another: Q4_0 given Q8_0 costs 3.7% of standalone (27x) [M]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2026-09-03, quant_given_quant.py, qgq/qgq.json. Every scheme in this programme has treated the quant
files of a repo as independent, and the GGUF lever so far has needed the BASE model plus a quantizer run
plus the right imatrix. This needs none of that, and it comes from the structure of the format itself.

THE ARITHMETIC. For a block of 32 weights ggml computes Q8_0 as d8 = amax/127 with codes q8 = round(x/d8),
and Q4_0 as d4 = max/-8 with q4 = round(x/d4), where amax is the block's largest magnitude and max is
that same element WITH ITS SIGN. Both scales are functions of the same block extreme, and x is known from
the first quantisation to within d8/2. So
    q4_predicted = round(q8 * d8/d4)
is wrong only where x falls within d8/2 of a Q4 rounding boundary -- a band of width (1/2)(8/127) = 3.1%
of a step. The prediction should therefore be right almost always, with a residual of at most +/-1.

MEASURED on bartowski/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct-GGUF, blk.0.ffn_gate.weight, 200,000 blocks (6.4M weights)
fetched as aligned byte ranges from the two published files:
    codes predicted exactly            98.85%
    residual                           98.85% zero, 100.00% within +/-1, range [-1, +1]
    scales exactly derivable            0.81%, but the difference is a small fp16 offset costing
                                        0.0550 bits/weight to code
    Q4_0 coded alone                    4.2570 bits/weight (code entropy 3.757 + the fp16 scale)
    Q4_0 as a residual on Q8_0          0.1570 bits/weight
    => 3.7% of standalone, a factor of 27.1
The residual being ENTIRELY within +/-1 is the signature of the mechanism rather than of a fitted model:
it is what the 3.1% boundary band predicts, and nothing else would produce it.

WHY THIS MATTERS MORE THAN THE RECIPE LEVER. Regenerating a quant from its base (RESULT 59, 72) needs the
base model resident, the exact llama.cpp release, the intermediate dtype, the imatrix and the build
flags, and it reaches the 82% of GGUF bytes whose base is named. This needs only ONE of the repo's own
files and arithmetic. Of the 35 sampled GGUF repos, 34 (97%) publish more than one quant file, holding
98% of the GGUF bytes, with a median of 15 files per repo and a maximum of 104.

Sizing it on those repos, keeping the largest quant whole and coding the rest as residuals:
    13.36 TB of quants -> 2.22 TB, i.e. 83% less, a factor of 6.0
(The per-file 27x does not become a 27x per repo because the one file kept whole dominates the result.)

NOT MEASURED, and it decides the reach: this is a LEGACY-format pair (Q4_0 given Q8_0). The K-quants --
Q4_K_M, Q5_K_M, Q6_K -- carry most of today's downloads and use super-blocks with their own 6-bit
sub-scales, so the same principle should hold (their scales are still functions of block extremes) but
the decoder is more involved and it has NOT been tested. Until it is, the 6.0x is demonstrated only for
repos whose quants are legacy types. Also unmeasured: the speed of reconstruction, and whether the
prediction survives across quant types produced by different llama.cpp releases.

RESULT 76 — the K-quants are predictable too: Q4_K_M given Q8_0 costs 13.4% of standalone (7.4x) [M]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2026-09-03, kquant_given.py, qgq/kqgq.json. RESULT 75 proved the mechanism on a LEGACY pair (Q4_0 given
Q8_0, 27x) and named the K-quants as the question that decides its reach, since Q4_K_M, Q5_K_M and Q6_K
carry most of the Hub's GGUF downloads and are built differently: a 256-weight super-block with a scale
and a minimum, eight sub-blocks each with a 6-bit scale and minimum, and 4-bit offsets from those.

The prediction follows from the DEQUANTISATION rule rather than the quantiser's search. Q4_K reconstructs
x = d_j*q - m_j, so given the sub-block's own stored d_j and m_j (16 of the block's 144 bytes) and an
approximation of x from another quantisation:  q_predicted = round((x + m_j)/d_j).

MEASURED on bartowski/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct-GGUF, blk.0.ffn_gate.weight, 40,000 super-blocks
(10.2M weights), both files fetched as aligned byte ranges:
    codes predicted exactly       99.06%
    residual                      99.06% zero, 100.00% within +/-1, range [-1, +1]
    Q4_K_M coded alone            4.3621 bits/weight
    Q4_K_M as a residual on Q8_0  0.5864 bits/weight
    => 13.4% of standalone, a factor of 7.44
Of the 0.5864, the residual codes are only 0.0864 bits/weight; the remaining 0.50 is the scales, minima
and the 12 packed scale bytes, which are still stored verbatim. Predicting THOSE (they are chosen by a
search over the same weights) is the obvious next step and would roughly halve the remainder again.

So the mechanism is general, not a legacy-format curiosity: 98.85% exact on Q4_0, 99.06% on Q4_K_M, and
in both cases a residual entirely within +/-1 -- the signature of the rounding-boundary band, not of a
fitted model.

POPULATION SIZING, at the conservative K-quant rate (13.4%, not the 3.7% legacy rate):
    multi-quant repos: 13.36 TB of quants -> 3.34 TB   (75% less, 4.0x)

    held today 1.540 PB -> 1.270 PB with mzip, 17.5% less

RESULT 77 — the plane split is wasted work on 8-bit data: same size, more speed without it [M]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Same day, on the 8 MiB Hub slices of RESULT 71, core 3, load 1.70, 5 reps. The coder de-interleaves the
input into k = 2 or 4 byte planes, which is meaningful for 16- and 32-bit elements and meaningless when
the element IS one byte: the planes are then just alternate elements, statistically identical, so the
gather and scatter are pure overhead on both paths.

    slice            plane coder (k=2/4)                    plain zstd-1 (no split)
    FP8 E4M3         1.2133, encode 762, decode 1192 MB/s   1.2132, encode 1210, decode 1456 MB/s
    INT8             1.7272, encode 919, decode 1969 MB/s   1.7282, encode 1237, decode 2337 MB/s

The ratios agree to four decimals -- 1.2133 vs 1.2132 and 1.7272 vs 1.7282 -- while encode is 1.35-1.59x
and decode 1.19-1.22x faster without the split. The equality is the load-independent part and is the
real finding; the speeds were taken on a quiet core to make them quotable.

Consequence: the coder should take k = 1 (code the byte stream directly) for 1-byte element types, and
the format's own k field already has room to say so. Applied to the 87 TB/week of 8-bit weights, this is
the same saving delivered at 1.2x the read speed, which moves FP8 from "wins below 1.7 Gb/s" toward
"wins below 2.1 Gb/s" without giving up a byte.

RESULT 78 — the K-quant scales are partly predictable too, and the imatrix is why only partly [M]

2026-09-03, kscale.py, qgq/kscale.json. RESULT 76 left Q4_K_M-given-Q8_0 at 0.5264 bits/weight, of which

RESULT 81 — the cost of rebuilding a quant, and why the scale prediction is archival-only [M]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2026-09-03, qgq_speed.py, qgq/speed.json. RESULTS 75-80 measured the SIZE of the quant-given-quant lever
and never its speed, which is the thing that decides where a store can put it. Two variants, timed on
the same 20,000 super-blocks (2.9 MB of Q4_K_M rebuilt from a Q8_0), numpy, core 3:

    variant                 throughput    the file costs    a 4 GB quant rebuilds in
    codes only               66.0 MB/s     0.5264 b/wt        61 s
    codes + scale search      1.3 MB/s     0.3044 b/wt        52 minutes

The scale search costs 51.8x the read time to save 42% of the residual, because predicting the scales
means re-running ggml's search at READ time: 21 candidate scales per sub-block, each a weighted
least-squares fit. Predicting only the codes is a single pass -- dequantise the source, divide by the
sub-block's own stored scale, add the residual, repack.

So the two results split by tier, and the earlier reporting conflated them:
  * CODES ONLY is the serving-path variant. 4.2x on the class, and a 4 GB file back in a minute.
  * CODES + SCALES is archival. 5.1x on the class, but nobody waits 52 minutes for a download.

THE WEEK, restated for each:
    codes only (serving)     0.272 PB saved | held 1.540 -> 1.268 PB | 17.6% less | 14.1 PB a year
    + scale search (at rest) 0.277 PB saved | held 1.540 -> 1.263 PB | 18.0% less | 14.4 PB a year
The difference between the tiers is 5 TB a week -- 2% of the saving -- which is the honest reason to
prefer the fast variant everywhere except deep archive. RESULTS 78 and 79 spent real effort on the
scales; the effort produced a good number and a bad trade, and both belong in the record.

CAVEAT, and it is a large one: these are numpy timings and therefore an upper bound on the cost. A C
implementation of the same arithmetic would be faster by an unmeasured factor, as the delta coder's
prototype was (33-57 MB/s in a binary range coder, 389/602 MB/s once written properly). What is
implementation-independent is the RATIO: the search does 21 passes where the codes path does one, so
the ordering of the two tiers will survive any rewrite even if both numbers move.

RESULT 83 — FP8 given its base: not a simple cast where AWQ is involved, and the instrument was wrong
twice before it could say so [M, partial]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2026-09-03, fp8_given_base.py, fp8_check.py, fp8b.py, fp8/fp8.json. Category 3 of the frame -- files that
are the output of a deterministic program over something the Hub already holds -- had been mined for one
program only, llama.cpp's quantiser. The 8-bit and 32-bit integer safetensors are 87 TB of the week and
had been treated as ordinary bytes, though they are quantisations too. The simplest hypothesis available
anywhere in this programme: an FP8 checkpoint is w_fp8 = round_e4m3(w_base / scale), with the scale
stored in the same file. No calibration, no search, no iteration.

FIRST ATTEMPT: 0.05-0.96% exact across six repos. That number was worthless, and the falsification pass
found two independent reasons:
  * the E4M3 encoder was WRONG. Decoding all 256 patterns and re-encoding them returned 226 of 240;
    every subnormal (0x01-0x07 and their negatives) collapsed to 0x08. An encoder that cannot reproduce
    the format's own values cannot test a hypothesis about it.
  * the scale model was wrong. The probe fitted ONE scale per tensor; real checkpoints store one per
    128x128 BLOCK -- a [5120, 17408] weight carries a [40, 136] scale tensor. A single scale could never
    have matched.
Both are the kind of error that produces a confident negative. Checking the instrument cost one script
and saved a false result; it is the same discipline that caught the 1 MiB GGUF header window earlier.

SECOND ATTEMPT, both fixed. The encoder is now built as an exact nearest-representable mapping derived
from the decoder itself, ties to even, and self-tests at 239/240 patterns (the remaining one is negative
zero). The scale is read from the file and broadcast over its 128x128 blocks. On
TelperionAI/Qwen3.8-27B-FP8-block-AWQ against its declared base Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B, same tensor, same
shape:
    w / scale      exact  1.21%    within one code  3.83%
    w * scale      exact  0.00%    within one code  0.00%

So the FP8 weights are not a cast of the declared base -- and the repo name says why. AWQ applies
activation-aware per-channel scaling to the weights BEFORE quantising, so the file is a cast of
transformed weights. This is the same mechanism measured in RESULT 59, where the official Qwen GGUFs
turned out to hold a function-preserving per-channel rescaling of the public weights rather than the
weights themselves. Two independent quantisation ecosystems, the same obstacle: the transform is applied
first and its scale vectors are not published.

WHAT IS AND IS NOT SHOWN: a PLAIN FP8 cast, with no AWQ or smoothing step, remains untested -- every
candidate the sampled population offered was either AWQ, had no safetensors, or had a shape mismatch
against its declared base. The hypothesis is alive for that case and dead for this one. The honest
position for the table is that the 87 TB of 8-bit weights keeps only its per-chunk saving (RESULT 71,
4-42%, free ground because Xet stores them raw) and gains nothing from category 3 until a
non-transformed FP8 pair is found and measured.

"scale 100.0%" on every row -- and never acted on. Q8_0 stores d = amax/127 as fp16 per 32 weights, and
amax comes from the SAME base weights the codes come from. A quantity reproduced exactly does not need

    type   n   scales exact   codes exact   cost, scales STORED   cost, scales PREDICTED
    Q8_0   5        100.00%        98.66%              7.7%                1.40%   (72x)
    Q6_K   8          0.15%       100.00%              8.9%                   --   (n/a)

keep paying their superblock overhead (0.500-0.562 bits per weight) and stay at 8.9-13.2%, and Q8_0
drops to 1.4%.

class rate moves from 0.105 to 0.075.

RESULT 95 — mismatched K-quant codes cluster at rounding ties, and the decoder can see the tie [M]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2026-09-03, tieprobe.py. Every residual in RESULTs 84-94 is coded at order-0: h0(resid) treats a miss
as equally likely at every position. But a miss is a rounding DISAGREEMENT, and rounding disagrees
where the pre-rounding value (x+M)/D sits near k+0.5. If misses cluster there, the residual is
conditionally predictable and the decoder pays h(resid | tie-distance) instead -- with NO side
information, because the decoder reconstructs (x+M)/D from the parent and the stored scales before it
reads the residual, so it knows the tie-distance too.

MEASURED, sibling Q8_0 -> K-quant (the within-repo route, RESULT 84), bartowski/Qwen2.5-1.5B, 16
tie-distance buckets:
    type   tensor                miss   h0(resid)  h(resid|tie)   gain   misses in nearest 1/16 to a tie
    Q4_K   blk.0.ffn_gate       0.94%     0.0865       0.0410    0.0454        98.8%   (chance: 6.25%)
    Q5_K   blk.0.ffn_gate       2.47%     0.1917       0.1012    0.0905        69.8%
    Q6_K   blk.0.ffn_down       5.18%     0.3456       0.2173    0.1283        44.0%

The clustering is not subtle: 98.8% of Q4_K misses sit in the sixteenth of values nearest a tie. Q6_K
clusters less because its codes are finer (63 levels) so the Q8_0 proxy's own rounding noise lands
misses further from ties -- the parent is lossy, and this measures that.

EFFECT ON COST. Total cost per weight is scale overhead + residual. Q4_K: 0.500 + 0.0865 = 0.587 ->
0.500 + 0.0410 = 0.541 (7.8% less). Q6_K: 0.562 + 0.3456 = 0.908 -> 0.562 + 0.2173 = 0.779 (14.2%
less). Across the sibling route this moves RESULT 84's 13.5% of standalone to roughly 12%. It does
NOT move the base route (RESULT 89), where Q5_K/Q6_K already reproduce exactly and there is no
residual to condition.

WHY THIS IS A LEVER AND NOT A TWEAK: it is the first evidence in this programme that a residual has
exploitable structure at all. Order-1 on the CODES is 0.00% (RESULT 76) because a quantiser spends its
codebook. But the RESIDUAL is not a quantiser output -- it is the rounding boundary of two
quantisations disagreeing, and that boundary is geometric. A context model keyed on tie-distance is
~20 lines in the decoder. The next question is whether the sign of the miss is also predictable from
which side of the tie the value fell (it should be, nearly always), which would take h(resid|tie)
close to the miss-position entropy alone.
